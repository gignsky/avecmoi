UAD 3.6 is here.

The biggest change to appraisal reporting in over fifteen years — one new report, far more detail, and a different vocabulary. Here is what it means for your listings, in plain English.

The one-sentence version

Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac replaced every legacy appraisal form with one dynamic report.

The familiar 1004 and its cousins are gone. In their place is the redesigned Uniform Residential Appraisal Report — the URAR — a single report that adapts to the property and the assignment. It becomes mandatory for new conventional appraisals on November 2, 2026.

One reportA single dynamic URAR replaces the whole stack of legacy forms.
Nov 2, 2026Mandatory for new appraisals on Fannie Mae & Freddie Mac loans.
Data-firstDelivered as structured data (XML) plus a readable PDF and a photo folder.
It changes how a home is described — not how it is valued.

UAD 3.6 reorganizes and standardizes the information in the report. It does not change the methods an appraiser uses to arrive at an opinion of value.

Where we are

The timeline

The mandate has arrived — but a one-time exception gives some lenders a tail into 2027, so you will still see both report formats for a while.

  1. Through now Broad production Lenders may submit UAD 2.6 or 3.6. You will see both, deal to deal.
  2. In effect Nov 2, 2026 UAD 3.6 required for new appraisals submitted for GSE loans — based on the submission date, not the application date.
  3. Exception tail Mar 1, 2027 Legacy 2.6 reports filed under an exception lose rep & warrant relief and take a top collateral-risk score.
  4. Final May 20, 2027 UAD 3.6 mandatory for everyone; the exception window closes May 19.

On September 30, 2026 the GSEs granted a one-time policy exception for lenders that could not finish in time — so a lender with an exception may keep filing the legacy format into mid-2027. Only lenders that sell directly to Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac request it; brokers do not. FHA is adopting UAD 3.6 as well (no firm date yet), and VA is expected to follow. The short version for you: during the transition, ask the lender which format applies.

Under the hood

What's actually changing

Six shifts worth knowing. None of them change the standard of value — they change how thoroughly the property is documented and how the data is structured.

1

One dynamic report

The URAR replaces the legacy form set. The old form number no longer sets the scope — the lender's engagement letter specifies the inspection level and property type, and one report flexes to fit.

2

Condition & quality, split out

The C1–C6 and Q1–Q6 scales are unchanged, but the definitions were rewritten for clarity — and the appraiser now rates condition and quality separately for the exterior, the interior, and overall, with room-level detail for each kitchen and bath. These ratings now apply to manufactured homes too.

3

Far more structured detail

Energy-efficient features, disaster-mitigation features, component-by-component update status, broadband availability, ceiling-height categories — captured as discrete data fields instead of being buried in narrative comments.

4

New measurement language

"Gross living area (GLA)" is retired. Finished area is now reported as above-grade finished area, broken out by level, with below-grade area reported separately — aligned to the ANSI Z765-2021 measuring standard.

5

A data-first file

The appraisal is delivered as a package — a structured XML data file, a human-readable PDF, and a dedicated folder of property photos — not a single flat PDF. Clean, specific data matters more than ever.

6

Same opinion of value

Worth repeating: the way an appraiser reaches a value hasn't changed. What changed is how the property is described and how the report is organized.

Read the new report

Same home, new words

Several familiar labels have been renamed to match the new data standard. Abbreviations are retired — terms are now spelled out in full. A few you will notice first:

You've always saidNow it reads as
Gross living area (GLA)→Above-grade finished area (by level)
Basement→Below-grade area (reported separately)

Location→Site influence

The 1004 (and the form stack)→The redesigned URAR (one dynamic report)

The part that matters to you

What it means for realtors

You don't need to learn how to write the new report — that's the appraiser's job. But the agents who understand it, prepare for it, and explain it to their clients will stand apart. Five moves:

  1. Document improvements precisely

    The report now has structured fields for what was updated, when, and how extensively. Vague listing language doesn't translate. Capture the what, the year, the scope, and the materials.

  2. Build an appraiser package

    You know more about the transaction than anyone researching it later ever will. Leave that knowledge behind: improvement dates, permits, a feature list, and the comps you'd point to.

  3. Set your clients' expectations

    The report will look different and use unfamiliar words. A quick heads-up to buyers and sellers turns "why does this look strange?" into "ah, that's the new format."

  4. Ask about the format during the transition

    Lenders may still submit 2.6 or 3.6 for a while. If a deal's timing could cross a deadline, ask the lender which format applies.

  5. Be the guide, not the blamer

    Agents who understand UAD 3.6 stop blaming the lender when an appraisal takes longer or asks for more detail — and become the person their clients call first.

Listing language, upgraded

Instead of
"Beautifully updated throughout."
Write
"2024 — Kitchen remodeled: cabinetry, countertops, and appliances."

Specifics now land in real data fields. Dates, scope, and materials do the work that adjectives used to.
